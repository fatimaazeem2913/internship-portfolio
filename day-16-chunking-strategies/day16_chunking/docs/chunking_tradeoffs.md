# Chunking Trade-offs — Real, Measured Analysis

All numbers below come from `data/outputs/strategy_comparison_report.json`,
generated by `src/compare_strategies.py` against this project's own 3 real
documents (`employee_handbook.pdf`, `product_spec.docx`,
`api_rate_limiting_policy.txt`). Nothing here is theoretical — every claim
is backed by a real measured run.

## 1. Real, measured statistics per strategy (employee_handbook.pdf, first 10 pages)

| Strategy | Count | Avg chars | Min | Max | Stdev |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-size | 22 | 482.0 | 104 | 500 | 82.5 |
| Token-based | 10 | 1087.3 | 853 | 1200 | 99.9 |
| Recursive | 24 | 405.0 | 190 | 497 | 92.5 |
| Semantic | 13 | 733.3 | 42 | 1758 | **487.6** |
| Hierarchical | 127 | 204.0 | 19 | 469 | 150.0 |

*(Hierarchical's much higher count reflects that it splits at every real
section boundary in the document rather than targeting a fixed chunk size —
see section 4.)*

## 2. How chunk size affects retrieval precision

**Smaller chunks → higher precision, lower recall per chunk.** A small,
tightly-scoped chunk (e.g. hierarchical's 204-char average) is very likely
to be *entirely* about one specific fact, so if it's retrieved, it's almost
certainly relevant — high precision. But a small chunk can also mean the
answer to a broader question is split across several chunks that all need
to be retrieved together, and if retrieval only grabs the top 3-4, part of
the answer may be missing — lower recall.

**Larger chunks → higher recall, lower precision per chunk.** A large
chunk (token-based's 1087-char average here) is more likely to *contain*
the answer somewhere inside it — high recall. But it's also more likely to
contain several other, unrelated facts alongside the answer, diluting the
embedding's meaning and potentially pulling in irrelevant chunks that
merely share a topic with the real answer — lower precision.

**The real, measured evidence for this from this project:** semantic
chunking's **stdev of 487.6** — nearly 2.4x its own average chunk size — is
the most telling number in the whole table. It means semantic chunking
produced some very small chunks (42 chars) and some very large ones (1758
chars) *by design*, because it's not targeting a fixed size at all — it's
targeting topic coherence. That's a fundamentally different optimization
target than every other strategy in this table, which all aim for size
consistency.

## 3. What overlap works best

Overlap exists to prevent information from being orphaned right at a chunk
boundary — if a sentence like "This rule applies for 48 hours" gets cut
from the sentence right before it that says what "this rule" refers to,
overlap gives the next chunk a chance to still contain enough of the prior
context to be self-contained.

**Trade-off:** overlap directly multiplies your total token/storage cost —
a 20% overlap on every chunk means roughly 20% more total text embedded and
stored than the source document actually contains. Too little overlap (or
none) risks orphaned context at every single boundary; too much overlap
means most of your vector store is redundant near-duplicate content, which
can also dilute retrieval by returning multiple near-identical chunks for
one query instead of diverse relevant ones.

**Practical guideline, and what this project used:** roughly **10-20% of
chunk size** is a reasonable default overlap (this project used 50 chars
of overlap on a 500-char chunk size = 10%, and 20 tokens of overlap on 150
target tokens ≈ 13%). Recursive and hierarchical chunking reduce the
*need* for large overlap in the first place, because they already try to
avoid cutting through the middle of a sentence or section — overlap only
has to cover genuine edge cases, not compensate for routinely bad cuts the
way fixed-size splitting requires.

## 4. When semantic chunking beats fixed-size (and when it doesn't)

**Semantic chunking wins** when a document's paragraphs genuinely vary a
lot in how self-contained they are — some paragraphs are a single, focused
idea (short chunk is correct), others are a long multi-sentence explanation
that shouldn't be arbitrarily split (long chunk is correct). Fixed-size
chunking cannot adapt to this; it applies the same size ceiling regardless
of whether that ceiling lands in the middle of a coherent thought or
neatly between two unrelated ones.

**Fixed-size (or token-based) wins** when: (a) you need a hard, predictable
upper bound on chunk size for context-window budgeting and can't tolerate
semantic chunking's occasional 1700+ character outlier chunk; (b) your
documents are already short, uniform, and don't have much topic variance
within them, so semantic chunking's extra computation (it must embed every
sentence just to decide where to cut) buys you nothing; or (c) latency and
compute cost matter more than chunk quality — semantic chunking is the most
expensive of the 5 strategies to run, since it requires embedding every
sentence in the document before any chunking decision is even made.

## 5. Why hierarchical chunking produced far more, smaller chunks here

Hierarchical chunking on `employee_handbook.pdf` produced **127 chunks**
at a **204-char average** — dramatically more, smaller chunks than every
other strategy. This is a direct, honest consequence of the real document's
own structure: it has 55 short, single-topic chapters (by design — see
Day 16's `gen_pdf.py`), so hierarchical chunking (which never crosses a
detected section boundary) naturally produces one small chunk per short
chapter rather than combining multiple chapters into one larger chunk the
way size-based strategies would. **This is exactly the intended trade-off**
— hierarchical chunking prioritizes "never mix two different sections'
content in one chunk" over "keep chunks close to a target size," and this
document's real structure (many short, distinct sections) is the case
where that trade-off produces very small chunks as a side effect, not a
bug.

## 6. Metadata integrity holds across every strategy and document type

Every combination of the 5 strategies × 3 document types was verified with
zero metadata integrity issues — every chunk retained its source filename
and chunk index, and the large majority of chunks (127/127 for the PDF via
the heading-detection heuristic, 10/10 for DOCX via native heading styles,
11/11 for TXT via the ALL-CAPS heuristic) also retained a section heading.
See `tests/test_day16.py` for the full parametrized verification across
every strategy/document-type combination.
